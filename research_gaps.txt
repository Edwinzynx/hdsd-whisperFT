1.3 Research Gaps

1. Language Coverage Scarcity (English-Centric Pathological Corpora)
   The current landscape of pathological speech recognition research remains heavily dominated by English-centric databases. Benchmarking resources such as the UASpeech [3] and TORGO [4] datasets provide extensive phonetic variety, yet their findings do not easily translate to low-resource, phonetically distinct languages like Hindi. Standard multi-task models, such as OpenAI's Whisper, exhibit low zero-shot performance on non-English disordered speech because their pretraining datasets are composed predominantly of clean, standard speech from high-resource languages [1]. Although the Hindi Dysarthric Speech Database (HDSD) introduced by Tripathy [6] represents a vital first step, the lack of extensive, multi-speaker Hindi corpora limits baseline ASR robustness and adaptation strategies, highlighting a severe language accessibility gap in assistive technologies.

2. Sentence Memorization Leakage in Dataset Splits
   Evaluation methodologies in pathological speech recognition frequently suffer from data leakage caused by dataset design. Typical speaker-independent splits isolate speaker identities but fail to control for sentence templates. As a result, the same sentence template appears in both training and testing datasets (spoken by different subjects), allowing the model's language decoder to memorize vocabulary patterns and template structures rather than learning generalized acoustic-phonetics [7]. This leakage results in artificially inflated accuracy metrics, with models demonstrating very low error rates on familiar templates but degrading significantly on unseen sentence structures. Resolving this requires a strict dual-independent split (disjoint speakers and disjoint sentence templates) to evaluate the model's true generalization capacity on unseen speech.

3. Severity-Level Performance Variability
   Pathological speech displays highly variable intelligibility levels depending on the clinical severity of the underlying condition. General fine-tuning approaches commonly pool mild, moderate, and severe dysarthric recordings together without severity-level stratification. This training methodology results in models overfitting to mild articulation patterns while failing to recognize the prolonged phonemes, slurred consonants, and variable speech rates characteristic of severe dysarthria [12]. Research indicates that ASR performance drops by 30% to 50% on severe cases unless models employ targeted training schemes, such as curriculum learning or acoustic rhythm conversion [7], [12].

4. Compute, Storage, and Deployment Constraints
   Adapting large-scale pre-trained models to pathological speech via full-parameter fine-tuning yields high transcription accuracy, but it introduces massive computational overhead and large model checkpoints. This resource requirement forms a deployment bottleneck for lightweight assistive applications, which must run locally on consumer devices to guarantee low latency and user privacy. Implementing parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods, such as Adaptive Low-Rank Adaptation (AdaLoRA) [7], or post-hoc techniques like checkpoint model merging [8], is necessary to adapt base models to atypical speech profiles without requiring extensive hardware resources or large storage capacities.

5. Cultural, Accents, and Code-Mixed Phonetic Variations
   ASR adaptation for the Indian demographic must address unique linguistic behaviors, such as regional accent variations and the frequent use of code-mixed Hindi-English (Hinglish). Pathological speech distortions, combined with these code-mixed patterns, introduce significant phonetic alignment challenges. Standard Hindi tokenizers and phonetic maps fail to align with the acoustic distortions produced by motor speech disorders, and prior works focusing on code-mixed custom tokenizers [11] are restricted to healthy speech. Adapting vocabulary mappings and language decoders to handle both regional acoustic accents and pathological phoneme distortions remains an unaddressed requirement for Indic assistive systems.

1.4.1 Problem Definition
The primary problem addressed in this project is the severe degradation of Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) systems when transcribing atypical speech produced by individuals with dysarthria. Standard state-of-the-art architectures, including multilingual foundations like OpenAI's Whisper, are optimized for typical speech articulation, clear phonetic structures, and uniform pacing. Consequently, when encountering dysarthric speech—which is characterized by slurred articulation, imprecise consonant voicing, prolonged vowels, breathiness, and irregular pauses—the word error rate (WER) climbs to critical levels (often exceeding 50% to 70%), rendering these interfaces unusable. 

This problem is further compounded by two major challenges:
1. Low-Resource Language Scarcity: High-quality, annotated dysarthric datasets are scarce, particularly for Hindi, where the only public corpus (the Hindi Dysarthric Speech Dataset - HDSD) is restricted in size and contains noisy Hinglish-to-Devanagari orthographic variations that corrupt reference keys.
2. Structural Data Leakage: Standard evaluations use speaker-independent splits that do not restrict sentence templates, allowing models to memorize language constructs (sentence memorization) instead of generalizing acoustic-phonetic mappings to unseen speech.

Therefore, this project defines the problem as the lack of a data-efficient, personalized, and robust Hindi ASR model capable of transcribing atypical Hindi speech without suffering from sentence memorization or high acoustic-phonetic transcription errors.

1.4.2 Project Scope
The scope of this project is bounded by the following parameters, defining the limits of research, model training, and deployment:

*   Target Language and Orthography: The system focuses exclusively on transcribing Hindi speech. It establishes a static 128-word Hinglish-to-Devanagari spelling correction dictionary (FIXED_WORD_MAP) to normalize transcripts and ensure 100% spelling accuracy.
*   Data Corpus and Microphone Configuration: The dataset is limited to the dysarthric recordings of the HDSD database from IIT Roorkee. Within this corpus, only recordings captured by the M2 close-talk microphone are utilized to minimize background acoustic noise and focus on pure pathological articulation.
*   Model Selection and Fine-Tuning Constraints: The model adaptation uses the "openai/whisper-small" (244M parameters) model as the base architecture. Training is optimized for consumer hardware (4 GB VRAM GPU constraints) using PyTorch, FP16 mixed precision, and gradient accumulation (effective batch size of 8).
*   Data Split Validation: True generalization is evaluated using a strict Dual-Independent Split methodology, ensuring that the train, validation, and test datasets have zero overlap in both speaker identities and sentence template IDs (H01 to H30).
*   End-User System Interface: The primary deliverable is a local web application built with Gradio and faster-whisper. The interface supports real-time microphone capture via the Web Audio API, color-coded word-level transcription confidence scoring (filtering entropy segments), and personalized LoRA adapter uploads to adapt the model to individual speech severity levels.

1.8.1 Data Collection and Preprocessing
The system utilizes the Hindi Dysarthric Speech Dataset (HDSD), comprising paired pathological audio recordings and noisy textual transcripts. To resolve variable recording environments and pronunciation differences, a standard audio-text preprocessing pipeline is implemented:
*   Acoustic Normalization: Raw waveforms are resampled to a standardized 16 kHz mono WAV format, segmented into shorter utterances (5–15 seconds), and normalized to remove silence and background hums.
*   Devanagari Key Correction: Raw textual transcripts are passed through a Hinglish-to-Devanagari static spelling dictionary to remove heuristic vowels and ensure standard Hindi orthography.

1.8.2 Data Augmentation for Low-Resource Learning
To prevent model overfitting on low-resource training splits, data augmentation strategies are applied to increase diversity without increasing computational complexity:
*   Perturbations: Speed perturbation (simulating slight variations in speaker articulation speed) and pitch shifting (simulating vocal tract variations).
*   Noise Injection: Additive noise injection to improve acoustic encoder robustness in noisy consumer environments.
*   Synthetic Augmentation: Text-to-dysarthric speech (TTDS) synthesis to generate synthetic pathological samples for low-resource phonemes.

1.8.3 Baseline Establishment and Model Fine-Tuning
*   Baseline Establishment: The pretrained "openai/whisper-small" model is evaluated on the split datasets to establish zero-shot Word Error Rate (WER) and Character Error Rate (CER) baselines.
*   Parameter-Efficient Adaptation: Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) layers are inserted into the multi-head attention blocks. The model is trained using a low learning rate, gradient accumulation, and early validation checkpoints.

1.8.4 Severity-Based Personalization
A classification module categorizes input audio into severity tiers (mild, moderate, severe) based on standard speech pathologists' FDA scales. The ASR decoding parameters are then dynamically adjusted for the identified severity profile.

1.8.5 Confidence-Aware Error Correction
Entropy confidence thresholding (entropy < 0.7) via WhisperX is applied at the word level to flag low-intelligibility or misaligned segments. These regions are rescored using a pretrained IndicBERT language model to correct contextual errors.

1.8.6 Query-Responsive Interface Integration
The transcribed output is sent to a Gradio-based interface, enabling the user to interact via microphone input, edit incorrect words, issue vocal correction commands, and upload a 5-minute calibration clip to personalize LoRA adapter weights.

2.1.1 Theory Associated With Problem Area
Dysarthria is a motor speech disorder resulting from neurological conditions such as cerebral palsy, Parkinson's disease, or stroke. The primary physiological deficit is impaired coordination of speech muscles, which manifests acoustically as slurred articulation, prolonged phonemes, abnormal pauses, reduced pitch variation, and unstable volume pacing. These acoustic anomalies create a significant mismatch with standard automatic speech recognition (ASR) systems, which expect clear phonetic structures and uniform speech rates. ASR adaptation strategies must reconcile these acoustically distorted, variable wave inputs with standard textual orthographies.

2.1.2 Existing Systems and Solutions
Modern speech systems leverage deep learning paradigms, self-supervised representations (e.g., wav2vec 2.0), or large-scale multilingual foundations (e.g., OpenAI's Whisper). Common adaptation methods include supervised model fine-tuning and speaker-adaptive training using specific LoRA checkpoints. However, historical benchmarks remain highly English-centric (e.g., UASpeech and TORGO), which restricts their applicability to other languages. Adaptations for low-resource Indic speech must address phonetic variations, code-mixed language structures (Hinglish), and dataset scarcity.

2.4 Risk and Problem Analysis

2.4.1 Risk Management and Mitigation Strategies
*   VRAM Out-of-Memory (OOM) Risk: Adapting large speech models on local consumer hardware is constrained by VRAM capacity. This risk is mitigated by using parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT/LoRA) which only trains small adapter weights, utilizing half-precision execution (FP16), and optimizing the training batch layout with a per-device batch size of 1 and a gradient accumulation threshold of 8.
*   Overfitting Risk: Fine-tuning a complex model on a low-resource dataset of under 2,000 samples creates a risk of acoustic overfitting. This is mitigated by implementing early stopping based on validation loss, setting low initial learning rates, and utilizing data augmentations (speed/pitch perturbation).

2.4.2 Critical Problems Encountered and Solutions
During implementation, four major technical challenges were encountered and resolved:
*   Corrupted Ground-Truth Reference Key (Broken Answer Key): 
    - Problem: Heuristic regex rules (such as dropping ending vowels "ii -> i" and "ei -> e") in the initial preprocessing corrupted 69% of the ground truth sentences, creating a false-bad baseline WER because the model produced correct Hindi transcriptions but was evaluated against misspelled reference transcripts.
    - Solution: Programmatically analyzed the corpus and extracted its 128 unique transliterated Hinglish vocabulary words. Replaced regex rules with a static dictionary mapping (FIXED_WORD_MAP) that maps Hinglish words to standard Devanagari Hindi spelling.
*   Sentence Memorization (Data Leakage via Splitting):
    - Problem: Initial speaker-independent splitting allowed the same sentence templates (H01 to H30) to leak between training and test sets. This allowed the decoder to memorize language structures rather than evaluate acoustic generalization, inflating test performance.
    - Solution: Implemented a strict Dual-Independent Split (disjoint speakers and disjoint sentence template IDs), ensuring the test set contains only unseen speakers speaking unseen sentence prompts.
*   Control Speaker Contamination:
    - Problem: Healthy control speaker transcripts (such as those starting with "C") leaked into the test set, inflating accuracy metrics.
    - Solution: Filtered out all control speakers starting with "C" from the training, validation, and testing partitions, ensuring the model is evaluated purely on dysarthric speech.
*   Hardware VRAM Memory Overflow:
    - Problem: Running full-parameter fine-tuning of Whisper-Small on a local 4 GB VRAM GPU triggered CUDA out-of-memory errors.
    - Solution: Switched to parameter-efficient fine-tuning (AdaLoRA) to adapt the multi-head attention layers without full-parameter tuning overhead, keeping checkpoints compact and execution memory within 4 GB bounds.

*   Acoustic Front-End Layer: Reads linear PCM audio input waveforms from the microphone, applies resampling to 16,000 samples per second, splits long waveforms into structured chunks of 30 seconds (standard Whisper context window), and computes 80-channel log-Mel spectrogram feature vectors from 25ms windows with a 10ms hop length.
*   Model Adaptation & Inference Layer: The Whisper Encoder stage processes the log-Mel spectrogram through a CNN feature extractor and a series of Transformer encoder blocks. The Decoder stage processes the encoder representation along with previous tokens, using parameter-efficient rank-adaptive matrices (AdaLoRA) on attention layers to adapt to slurred speech articulation. The Token Predictor then performs beam search decoding to generate Unicode Devanagari text tokens.
*   Post-Processing & UI Feedback Layer: WhisperX entropy alignment aligns the transcript at the word level and calculates token-level entropy. Words exceeding the entropy threshold of 0.7 are flagged as "low confidence" and passed to IndicBERT. IndicBERT rescores the candidate tokens based on contextual probabilities, correcting spelling or grammatical errors. Finally, the interface renders the output, color-coding high-confidence words in green/white and low-confidence words in red/orange.

4.3 User Interface Diagrams
The interface is designed for accessibility, featuring clear layout boundaries and minimum interaction overhead for motor-impaired individuals:
*   Audio Input Panel: Consists of an interactive microphone record button and an alternative drag-and-drop file upload zone. Includes a real-time waveform visualizer to show audio signal amplitude.
*   ASR display: Shows the transcribed Devanagari text. Standard words are displayed in standard black/white, while words below the confidence threshold are highlighted with a soft red/yellow background. Hovering over a highlighted word shows alternative word candidates.
*   Correction Panel: Supports "Tap-to-Edit" where users tap any word to open an inline text edit box. Includes vocal commands such as "repeat last sentence" or "edit last word".
*   Personalization Card: An upload widget allowing the user to upload a 5-minute audio calibration file to dynamically adapt model weights on-the-fly.

5.1 Work Accomplished
The project has successfully achieved all core milestones outlined in the approved objectives:
*   Orthography Normalization and Preprocessing: Programmed and executed a data cleaning pipeline that resamples all audio clips to a standardized 16 kHz mono WAV format, normalizes signal amplitudes, and maps transliterated Hinglish text transcripts to standard Devanagari Unicode Hindi characters using a static dictionary (FIXED_WORD_MAP).
*   Dual-Independent Splits Layout: Designed and generated strict dual-independent train, validation, and test datasets. The split consists of 899 training samples, 44 validation samples, and 45 testing samples, with complete separation in both speaker identities and sentence template IDs. All healthy control speakers were filtered out to maintain data integrity.
*   Baseline Evaluation: Established initial benchmark metrics on the dual-independent split using the pretrained "openai/whisper-small" model under zero-shot conditions, registering the baseline Word Error Rate (WER) and Character Error Rate (CER).
*   Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning Run: Executed supervised fine-tuning of Whisper-Small using rank-adaptive low-rank adaptation layers (AdaLoRA/LoRA PEFT modules) targeting multi-head attention weights. The training was optimized for a local 4 GB VRAM GPU environment using mixed-precision (FP16) calculations and gradient accumulation, with checkpoints saved successfully.
*   Interface Integration: Completed the development of the browser-based client application in Gradio, wrapping faster-whisper C++ inference models to perform real-time, low-latency transcriptions on local CPUs.

5.2 Conclusions
*   Acoustic Adaptation Viability: Fine-tuning speech models using parameter-efficient adapters represents an effective methodology for adapting large-scale multilingual ASR systems (like Whisper-Small) to the distorted phonemes, imprecise voicing, and pacing variability characteristic of dysarthric speech.
*   Dataset Integrity Constraint: Standard speaker-independent splits allow template leakage to inflate model accuracy, yielding artificially low error rates on seen sentence structures. Enforcing disjoint speaker and disjoint sentence partitions is necessary to evaluate the true generalizability of the model on unseen speech.
*   Orthographic Alignment Importance: Evaluating ASR models against inconsistent Romanized/Hinglish reference transcripts corrupts accuracy metrics. Defining standard dictionary spelling conventions is critical to evaluate phonetic mapping quality.

5.3 Environmental / Economic / Social Benefits
*   Social Benefits: The project enhances digital accessibility for individuals with motor-speech impairments by providing a personalized transcription tool. This empowers dysarthric individuals to access standard speech interfaces, write documents, and communicate with clinicians, speech therapists, and family members.
*   Economic Benefits: The local-first, on-device architecture minimizes dependency on cloud servers and subscription fees. The use of parameter-efficient training and CTranslate2 model serialization allows deployment on consumer-grade hardware (local CPUs and low-end GPUs), reducing computational hosting overhead.
*   Environmental Benefits: Local, parameter-efficient training drastically reduces carbon footprint compared to full-parameter model fine-tuning. On-device execution avoids the continuous energy consumption associated with hosting cloud servers, promoting energy-efficient AI deployment.

5.4 Future Work Plan
*   Scaling and Distillation: Scale the adaptation pipeline to larger foundational architectures (such as "openai/whisper-medium" or "whisper-large-v3") to analyze capacity gains. Apply knowledge distillation to compress model weights into highly compact representations (distilled Whisper checkpoints under 50 MB) suitable for edge deployment on mobile and wearable assistive devices.
*   Robust Augmentation: Implement real-time noise perturbation during training, adding typical household background noises (fan hums, television voices) to improve acoustic encoder robustness in noisy home-care settings.
*   Interactive Dynamic Adaptation: Expand the client-side adapter customization module, allowing real-time, interactive reinforcement learning where the model updates its speaker-specific weights incrementally based on user corrections.
